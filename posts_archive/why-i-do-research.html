My Motivation for Scientific Research

Minh-Hieu Nguyen May 2026 7 min read
"The detour is not wasted if it teaches you what the shortcut never could."

Someone once asked me: "Why do you do research?" — and I realized I had never really sat down to answer that question properly. Not for a grant application, not for a thesis introduction, but for myself. So here I am, trying to figure it out in writing.

The honest answer is that I don't fully know. What I do know is that I've always been drawn to the process of understanding — not just getting results, but understanding why things work the way they do. That feeling when a concept suddenly clicks, when a messy equation collapses into something elegant, when a graph starts to make sense — that's what keeps me going. Not the publications, not the credentials. The click.

· · ·

I fell in love with computational physics early on. The idea that you could model reality inside a computer, predict how atoms move, simulate how materials behave — it felt like the closest thing to magic I'd ever encountered. So when I got the chance to work at Academia Sinica in Taiwan, doing DFT calculations and neural network potentials under Prof. Jer-Lai Kuo, I thought I'd found my path.

I was wrong. Or rather — I was right about the destination but wrong about how ready I was for it.

The truth is, I struggled. The gap between what I knew and what the work demanded was enormous. The codebase was complex, the theory was deep, and everyone around me seemed to move at a speed I couldn't match. I didn't fail spectacularly — I just felt like I was constantly drowning in shallow water. Close enough to the surface to breathe, but never able to stand.

There's a particular kind of exhaustion that comes not from working too hard, but from feeling like you're not good enough for the work you love. That's what I carried back to Vietnam.

So I came home. Not as a retreat, but as a reset. I told myself I needed to build a stronger foundation — to fill the gaps that had left me gasping at Sinica. I needed to understand the experimental side of the physics I wanted to compute.

· · ·

That's how I ended up in Assoc. Prof. Phan Thế Long's group at VNU-UET, working on magnetocaloric effects in manganite nanoparticles. A completely different world from computational chemistry. Instead of writing code, I was weighing powders on a precision balance. Instead of debugging simulations, I was grinding samples in an agate mortar — by hand, for hours, until my arms ached and the powder was fine enough.

The solid-state reaction process became my daily life: weigh the precursors in the right molar ratios, grind them, calcine, grind again, press into pellets, sinter at high temperature, then characterize with XRD. Each step takes time. Each mistake costs days, not seconds. There's no "undo" button in a lab.

And I'll be honest — I felt lost again. Not because the work was bad. Prof. Long is an excellent researcher, and the science is fascinating. Tricritical behavior, the boundary between first-order and second-order phase transitions, Arrott plot analysis — these are beautiful problems. But every day, I was working in a field I hadn't trained for, using techniques I was still learning, and the old familiar feeling crept back in: I'm not enough for this.

The fear wasn't about breaking a sample. It was about spending years on a detour that leads nowhere.

When you're in a lab and you don't yet have intuition — when you don't know by feel how long to grind, what a good XRD pattern looks like at a glance, whether the sintering temperature needs adjusting — every step feels like walking on thin ice. You follow the protocol, but you can't feel it yet. And that gap between knowing the steps and owning them? That's where the anxiety lives.

· · ·

Here's what I've started to realize, though. And I want to write this down before I forget it, because on bad days I will need to read it again.

I didn't leave computational physics. I'm building the foundation to come back to it stronger.

Every scientist dreams of bridging theory and experiment. I knew this even as an undergraduate — that the best computational work is grounded in experimental reality. The people who write the most impactful simulations are the ones who have held the materials in their hands, who know what the data looks like before the computer touches it, who can smell when a result is physically unreasonable.

The agate mortar isn't a detour. It's tuition. Every hour I spend grinding Nd₀.₇Sr₀.₃MnO₃ powder is an hour that future-me will be grateful for — the version of me who writes Monte Carlo simulations of phase transitions and actually knows what the experimental curves are supposed to look like.

The magnetocaloric effect sits right at the intersection I've always been drawn to: condensed matter physics, statistical mechanics, materials science, and real-world applications like magnetic refrigeration. The tricritical point — where the phase transition changes its fundamental nature — is the kind of problem that doesn't yield to simple formulas. You have to really understand it. And understanding it from both sides, experiment and computation, is rare. That's the researcher I want to become.

· · ·

So why do I do research?

Not because I'm good at it. Most days, I feel like I'm not. Not because it's easy — it's the hardest thing I've ever done. Not because I have a clear five-year plan — I barely have a clear five-month plan.

I do research because I can't stop wanting to understand things. Because when I read a paper about critical exponents and something shifts in my brain, that feeling is worth all the hours of grinding powder and debugging code and staring at XRD patterns I can't yet interpret. Because I believe — maybe naively, maybe stubbornly — that the detour I'm on right now is not wasted. That every experiment teaches me something no textbook could. That one day, I'll sit in front of a simulation and know, in my bones, whether the result makes physical sense.

I do research because I can't stop wanting to understand. That's the whole answer. Everything else is just logistics.

If you're reading this and you're in a similar place — feeling lost in your own field, wondering if you chose wrong, scared that you're not enough — I don't have a solution for you. I'm still looking for mine. But I think the fact that we're still here, still showing up to the lab or the desk or the terminal, still asking "why?" when we could have stopped — that has to count for something.

The mortar and pestle is heavy. But my hands are getting stronger.

Like swinging a badminton racket, every motion makes me stronger.

Minh-Hieu Nguyen

Researcher at VNU-University of Engineering and Technology. Writing about physics, work-life balance, and the human side of science.
